Chorale Dice

A companion to the Musical Dice Game. Two dice for each of sixteen chords choose the harmony from a table; the page then writes soprano, alto, tenor and bass by the classical rules of voice leading, and sings them.

The sixteen chords

    How it works

    This is not a historical game. The eighteenth-century dice games, the one attributed to Mozart among them, worked with finished bars of keyboard music; nobody published one for four voices. This page borrows the idea and applies it a level down: the dice choose harmonies, and the voices are written by rule.

    There is a table of sixteen columns, one per chord, and eleven rows, one per dice total. Each column keeps to one harmonic function, so any throw produces two four-bar phrases: the first opens on the tonic and closes on the dominant at position 8, the second closes with a dominant and a tonic at positions 15 and 16. Those cadence columns are fixed; in the others the likely totals (6, 7, 8) carry the plainest chords and the rare ones (2 and 12) the colour, a borrowed minor subdominant or a secondary dominant.

    Given the sixteen chords, the four parts are chosen among every voicing that keeps each voice in its range, keeps the chord complete, doubles sensibly and never doubles the leading tone. The cheapest path through them is found where the cost between chords encodes the textbook: no parallel fifths or octaves, no hidden ones in the outer voices, the leading tone and every seventh resolve by step, raised notes rise and lowered notes fall, voices move as little as they can and never overlap. The same chords in the same key always give the same voices, so a link reproduces the chorale exactly.

    The sound is four sustained voices synthesised in the browser; the score is engraved from the same notes.
